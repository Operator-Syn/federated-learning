## 1. Introduction

Medicines play a vital role in the treatment and care of populations suffering from medical conditions. To meet the medication demand, the hospital supply chain (HSC) is managed from planning and quantifying needs to distributing medicines from pharmaceutical suppliers to hospital pharmacy (HP) and point-of-care locations (POCs) within a hospital (Neve & Schmidt, 2022;Shang et al., 2022). HSC also retrieves and accounts for expired and unused medicines (Dalalah et al., 2022;Ketzenberg et al., 2018;Priyan & Mala, 2020). In recent times, a significant rise has been observed in global HSC revenue. In the year 2022, the global market generated a revenue of USD 2.0 billion and is estimated to gain a revenue of USD 5.0 billion by the end of 2035 (Research Nester, 2023). The growth of the HSC market is primarily because of the higher number of people accessing medical support, medicines, and medical products.

The scope of HSC is not just the end-to-end distribution process of medicines, but also the element of human life and well-being of the society (Hussain et al., 2018;Khosravi & Izbirak, 2019;Zepeda et al., 2016). Yet HSC suffers from drug shortages, high cost, uncertainties, and the current COVID-19 pandemic has further worsened the situation (Ahmadi et al., 2022;Zepeda et al., 2016). HSC finds it challenging to manage the flow of medicines in a multi-echelon environment due to multiple stakeholders, lack of information sharing, and demand and lead time variability increasing in multiple stages (Govindan et al., 2023;Li et al., 2021;Shang et al., 2022). Such complexities present a strong reason for focusing on an essential class of HSC problem, i.e., inventory management (IM) in HSC. IM systems ensure that medicines of the right quality are available in the right quantity, at the right time, at the right location, and at minimum cost (Bozkir et al., 2022;Karamshetty et al., 2022;Neve & Schmidt, 2022). IM ensures that nurses and physicians can access the required medical products without any delay and dedicate their valuable time in taking care of the patients (Saha & Ray, 2019b).

However, the complexities of IM in HSC are unique, critical, and noteworthy to mention: Firstly, each patient arriving in a hospital are heterogeneous in nature, suffering from either single or multiple diseases, and consequently, need one or more medicines in different forms (oral/injection), dosages, categories, and combinations (Held et al., 2017). Because of this, it is extremely difficult for HSC to predict demand for medicines and make replenishment decisions. Secondly, in the hospitals, medicines are stored in multiple locations like HP and various POCs. This causes some intricacies like inaccurate information sharing, delay in distribution of medicines from HP to POCs, patients waiting for medicine leading to deterioration in their health, etc. (Saha & Ray, 2017, 2018;Vila-Parrish et al., 2012). Besides, any change in patient health condition may change the prescribed medicines ensuing return of earlier prescribed medicines from POCs to HP (Taleizadeh et al., 2020). In circumstances where many medications are administered, a lack of any supporting medications causes treatment to be delayed (and causes an accumulation of main medication inventory). This is because, here, both the medicines (main and supporting) need to be administered to patients in combination at the same time (i.e., causing demand dependence or association among medicines) (Saha & Rathore, 2022b;Yoosofan et al., 2015). This is the most unique feature of medicine IM, among many others. Apart from these issues, in hospitals, patient arrivals are highly random, which makes the medication demand in the HP and multiple POCs uncertain and stochastic in nature (Alexopoulos et al., 2008;Rosales et al., 2020;Saha & Rathore, 2022a). Besides placing emergency orders at additional cost, limited storage space at multiple locations, excess medicine inventory leading to wastage and disposal/return issues are also challenges for IM in HSC (Taleizadeh et al., 2020). Based on the identified research issues, the current study attempts to address the following research concerns.

RQ1: Does the knowledge of demand dependence among multiple medicines beneficial for IM in HSC?

RQ2: What will be the optimal inventory control policies for both the HP and POCs considering uncertain demand and demand dependence among medicines? RQ3: Does intelligent IM approaches in HSC better than the traditional techniques?

The main contributions and novelties of this research are as follows:

• With consideration for both the HP and the various POCs within the hospital, this study aims to identify the best inventory control policy for the HSC. • The investigation considers the demand dependence among prescribed medicines while estimating the demand for medicines in HSC. • This research extends the traditional inventory control policies like min-max and can-order policy by developing a new policy considering the associations among medicines.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows: Section 2 comprehensively reviews the related literature, highlighting the research gap. Section 3 formulates a mathematical model along with a solution approach. Section 4 presents a case study to validate the mathematical model and analyze sensitive parameters. Section 5 discusses the managerial insights and practical implications. Finally, the conclusions, limitations and future research directions are included in Section 6.

## 2. Literature review

Identifying replenishment decisions to balance supply and demand in a resource-constrained context is the goal of the class of problems known as IM in the HSC. The HSC has released a substantial body of material on IM. This section lists pertinent research on IM in HSC as well as specifically on IM in HP and POCs. The aim is examining similar studies and highlighting the current research gap in this field.

### 2.1. IM in HSC

Many studies are conducted on IM in HSC and have suggested IM approaches for different healthcare products like medicines, medical supplies, vaccines, surgical instruments, etc. Recently, a few studies have explored multi-echelon IM for improving the HSC coordination (Vanbrabant et al., 2023;Kochakkashani et al., 2023;Kamran et al., 2023). Besides, some of the multi-echelon IM models in HSC have also focused on multiple medical items, however, under independent demand among items (Silva-Aravena et al., 2020;Bozkir et al., 2022). Silva-Aravena et al. (2020) identified rolling horizon approach for managing flow of medicine inventory from supplier to HP at minimum cost under uncertain demand while Bozkir et al. (2022) identified an inventory pooling approach for managing flow of medicine inventory from supplier to multiple hospitals to achieve maximum service level under uncertain demand and supply. Studies by Ahmadi et al. (2022) and Modak et al. (2020) considered the flow of medicine inventory from suppliers to warehouse to multiple hospitals. Interestingly, Lotfi et al. (2022aLotfi et al. ( , 2022b) ) dealt with medical IM in COVID-19 conditions and found vendor managed inventory policy as an effective method for controlling and lowering inventory expenses. The authors created a robust optimization model that combines fuzzy and data-driven aspects for improving the IM system for tackling demand uncertainty. Further, Masoudi and Mirzazadeh (2022) created an innovative method to address the inventory model in which the lead times and stochastic deterioration rates arise in the inventory system of a single pharmaceutical supplier with several hospitals. To overcome the issue, the writers used genetic algorithms and particle swarm optimization. Broadly, HSC includes the flow of healthcare products from the supplier to the HP as well as from the HP to the several POC inventory locations within the hospital (Saha & Ray, 2019a). In such cases, inventory control policies are required both for the HP and for each of the POCs. The subsequent sub-sections will review explicitly the IM systems in HP and POCs.

### 2.2. IM in HP

The HP stores a large volume and great variety of medicines, checks on-hand inventory of medicines, and meets the demand of all the POCs (Bialas et al., 2020;Gawronska & Kolinski, 2022;Silva-Aravena et al., 2020). HP also places and receives orders from multiple pharmaceutical suppliers. Prior studies by Saha and Ray (2019b) considered the patient condition-based IM in HP and used Markov decision process model to find the optimal replenishment policy under uncertain medication demand. The authors found the patient condition-based (s, S) policy to be the optimal. This policy states that when the inventory level of the medicine in HP is at reorder point (s), an order will be placed to bring it up to order-up-to level (S). Baloch and Gzara (2020) investigated several issues pertaining to the selection and placement of pharmaceuticals in the HP. The writers examined sales of pharmaceuticals, ascertained the best levels of service and storage capacity, and suggested guidelines for supplier-driven product substitution, selection, and stocking. Using sales data, the authors created several mixed integer optimization models that produce reliable results while considering demand unpredictability. Fernandez et al. (2021) developed a data-driven model to boost the effectiveness of HP's inventory management strategy. An IM system that gave order quantities required to satisfy clinical needs and the risk of stockout if no order is placed for various time horizons helped the HP staff. Overall, most of the earlier studies use the exact method, heuristics, and simulation to solve the inventory models in HP.

### 2.3. IM in POCs

In hospitals, the demand for medicines is generated at multiple POCs (such as emergency rooms, operating rooms, intensive care units, high dependence units, wards, etc.) (Neve & Schmidt, 2022;Rosales et al., 2014). The POCs process medication orders, store limited medicines, dispense prescribed medicines to patients, and place orders to HP (Bijvank & Vis, 2012;Rosales et al., 2015). Prior studies by Bijvank and Vis (2012) developed two types of exact models, viz., capacity model and the service level model for IM in POCs. The authors also formulate approximation models applicable for lost-sales inventory system (cost objective, no lead time restrictions, etc.). Rosales et al. (2014) examined the POCs' hybrid inventory management approach, which avoids expensive stockouts by combining a high-cost continuous replenishment option with a low-cost periodic replenishment cycle. The authors used simulation to create a parameter search engine that maximizes the longterm average cost per unit of time. Further, Rosales et al. (2015) developed the 2Bin inventory system under periodic and continuous review for efficiently managing vast inventories of medical supplies in POCs. Another study by Rosales et al. (2020) suggested joint replenishment policy to be beneficial for multi-product IM in POCs. Interestingly, (Bhosekar et al. 2023) examined the IM of surgical instruments in POC (operating rooms of hospital). Simulation models are developed comparing no coordination, partial coordination as well as full coordination of material handling and IM operations. Neve and Schmidt (2022) created models with the assumption of a periodic review, base-stock inventory policy with full backordering, and conditioned on the degree of accuracy in a specific POC inventory location, optimizing cost and service level.

Overall, to deal with the challenges in IM in HSC, HP and POCs, different approaches, including multi-criteria-multi-objective approach (Babaei et al., 2023), fuzzy programming (Nahofti Kohneh et al., 2023;Lotfi et al., 2022a), stochastic programming (SP) (Wang & Chen, 2022;Lotfi et al., 2024), robust programming (Shang et al., 2022;Lotfi et al., 2022b) and Markov decision process (MDP) (Aghsami et al., 2021), have been developed in the literature (refer to the recent review papers of Vanbrabant et al. (2023); Friday et al. (2021); Saha and Ray (2019) for the developed approaches). Such approaches, however, have certain restrictions. For example, many of these methods have not been created expressly for multi-item environments with uncertain demand as well as under demand dependence among multiple items. In fact, the traditional models are not developed for the multi-echelon environment with multiple decision makers where inventory control policies for multiple locations (like HP and all the POCs within a hospital) can be obtained. Realization of real-time demand and supply data and developing datadriven models are the need of the hour that can solve the IM problems in HSC in a smart way. Specifically, with the recent advancements of data mining techniques (such as association rule mining (ARM)) (Saha & Rathore, 2022b) and artificial intelligence/machine learning techniques (such as supervised, unsupervised and reinforcement learning (RL)) (Ahmadi et al., 2022), IM in HSC are shifting from the traditional models toward more intelligent systems (Weißhuhn & Hoberg, 2021).

From Table 1 it can be observed that to the best of our knowledge, no study has explored the inventory control policies both for the HP as well as for each of the POC locations within the hospital. No study has considered the return of medicines from the POC locations to the HP. No study has considered the demand dependence among multiple medicines and a very few studies have considered expediting/emergency orders (Kochakkashani et al., 2023;Modak et al., 2020). Expediting is important for hospitals to ensure patient demand fulfillment during stock-out situations. Generally, hospitals place emergency orders either to nearby hospitals or local suppliers at additional cost to meet the demand and avoid any delay in treatment (Rosales et al., 2020;Saha & Ray, 2019b).

Most of the studies in Table 1 have considered inventory control policies as stationary periodic review (Ahmadi et al., 2022;Bijvank & Vis, 2012) and continuous review replenishment policy (Fernandez et al., 2021;Rosales et al., 2014). However, no study has explored the non-stationary as well as joint replenishment policy which are most appropriate for the IM in HSC under stochastic and multi-item environment. Overall, numerous studies have developed mathematical programming models (such as MIP, NLP, and SP) for IM in HSC (Bozkir et al., 2022;Silva-Aravena et al., 2020), but a few studies have explored the decision-making under uncertainty models like MDP and semi-MDP (Rosales et al., 2015;Bijvank & Vis, 2012). Most of the studies have applied either exact methods, heuristics, or simulation to solve the IM problems in HSC (Bhosekar et al., 2023;Fernandez et al., 2021). Recently, studies by Kochakkashani et al. (2023), Ahmadi et al. (2022) and Saha and Rathore (2022) have discussed intelligent IM approaches in HSC, specifically the K-means clustering, RL, and ARM technique but independently. Overall, from Table 1, no study has integrated all the mentioned elements necessary for IM in HSC along with the recent intelligent techniques to mitigate the real-world challenges in IM. All these aspects show the clear gap in the existing literature and are the motivation for the present study.

## 3. Problem statement

The HSC under study considers that multiple pharmaceutical suppliers deliver medicines to the HP. The medicines are then delivered to various POCs (like intensive care units, high dependency units, general wards, etc.) within the hospital and administered to patients as shown in Fig. 2. Both the HP and POCs maintain inventory of medicines based on their demand and limited storage capacity. Fig. 2 illustrates that to meet the medication demand each of the POCs requests HP to issue medicines. To meet the requested orders of all the POCs, HP requests and places orders with various external pharmaceutical suppliers. In such HSC, the optimal inventory control policies are necessary both for HP and each of the POCs.

### 3.1. Assumptions

Since the patient's arrivals in a hospital are random and their medical conditions vary with time, we consider that the demand for medicines faced by each POCs is uncertain and stochastic in nature ( Schmidt, 2022). Due to multiple medical conditions, combinations of medicines are prescribed to patients by the physicians so there exists demand dependence or associations among prescribed medicines (Saha & Rathore, 2022b). Apart from that the hospital receives economic benefit for coordinating the ordering policy among different medicines (Liu et al., 2022). Hence, a joint replenishment policy is followed by the HP and each of the POCs considering demand dependence among multiple medicines. We measure the demand dependence level by identifying the association rules among medicines from the prescription data using ARM technique, and then using the confidence values (Park & Seo, 2013). The confidence values of an association rule that medicine set m is associated with medicine set m ′ (m⇒m ′ ) is defined as the ratio of prescriptions containing both medicine sets m and m ′ out of all the prescriptions containing medicine set m (Saha & Rathore, 2022b). Besides we consider that medicines have an expiration date and leftover or unused medicines at POCs can be returned to HP. Emergency orders can also be placed by HP to the local suppliers or nearby hospitals. In fact, POC units can also place emergency orders to HP and such deliveries are made immediately and instantaneously at any time. Further, there is a limited storage capacity and target service level at HP and each POCs for each medicine.

### 3.2. Mathematical model

A stochastic model (semi-MDP) is used to account for the pharmaceutical demand uncertainties and variabilities in the HSC since patient arrivals and their medical conditions are uncertain. Semi-MDPs describe sequential decision-making problems in stochastic environments Compared with MDPs, semi-MDPs have broader applications in realworld IM situations because they have more general sojourn-time distributions (Rosales et al., 2015). Hence, we model the stochastic inventory problem to determine the inventory control policies for HP and POCs using a semi-MDP. The state of the system is the combination of inventory levels at HP and POCs for all medicines. Let I = ( I HP mt , I mgt ) represents a set of all states in the semi-MDP model, where I HP mt represents the on-hand inventory level of medicine, m in HP at period, t, and I mgt represents the inventory level of medicine, m in each POC, g at period, t. Decisions or actions need to be made on whether to place an order to replenish the stock. The decisions or actions are represented as A = ( a HP mt , a mgt ) where a HP mt represents the order quantity of medicine, m in HP at period, t, and a mgt represents the inventory levels of medicine, m in each POC, g at period, t. Based on the decisions, the system undergoes a transition with an associated probability after a certain time. Let for each POC, g. Each transition has an associated ordering cost, holding costs for the time spent in a state, expediting cost for emergency orders in case of stock-out situations, expiration cost at the final period for the expired inventory, and return cost for the unused inventory.

Our objective is to minimize the incurred costs without sacrificing the service level. In HSC, patient demand needs to be fulfilled without any delay to avoid any fatal consequences. The service level maintained by the hospitals is very high to ensure that the probability of stock-out is negligible and the demand is fulfilled from the available stock (Saha & Ray, 2019b). Hence, in our study we aim to minimize the expected total inventory cost under service level constraints along with other conditions such as stochastic demand, demand dependencies among medicines, and limited storage capacity at HP and each POCs. ### 3.3. Solution approach

Generally, RL algorithms are the main solution methods for solving semi-MDPs (Wang & Liu, 2023). RL approaches can be learnt by ongoing interaction with the environment and are derived from AI/ML and dynamic programming (DP). For large-size semi-MDP models, the RL algorithms have also been shown to be very effective simulation-based stochastic approaches. We have created a multi-agent RL algorithm to solve the semi-MDP model. The agents are instructed to make the best choices for inventory replenishment as they represent the HP and the numerous POCs in the hospital. The inventory level of the medicine of that agent at time t is used to characterize each agent's state. The amount of medication that will be ordered by each agent at time t is specified as that agent's action. Agents visit a state at time t, perform an activity there, and then move on to another state. The agents pay a price right away because of the action. The Q-learning process, used by the multi-agent RL approach, records the Q-value, represented by Q(I, A), for each state-action pair. ## 4. Results

The proposed model is validated using data from the hospital information system of a multispecialty hospital in eastern India. Over the course of our 12-month period of data collection, we gathered medical data of almost 25,000 patients who were admitted to the hospital. The medication order information is explicitly extracted from the system. Each medication order is initiated by physicians for individual patients admitted in the hospital. It consists of the following information: date and time, patient name, patient identification number, name of prescribing physician, medication order identification number, medicine name and dose, patient medical condition and diagnosis, and type of POCs where the patient is admitted. The data consisted of approximately 28,000 unique medication orders of around 3,350 different medicines. The raw data collected from the hospital is confidential and is not shared.

Currently, the IM system in the case study hospital for all the medicines follows the (s, S) policy, where the policy parameter s is the reorder point and S is the order up-to-level point. The policy parameter values are presently decided by the hospital inventory managers and hospital pharmacists based on their experience and annual consumption. However, the mismatch between medication demand and manually determined medicine replenishment quantity (supply) is a primary indicator of the inefficiency of the current inventory policy. There have been instances where demand for medicines exceeded availability in HP and select POCs, resulting in out-of-stock conditions. Similarly, overestimation of replenishment quantity occurs in HP and specific POCs for some medications when supply outpaces demand. ### 4.2. Comparing model

This section examines the effectiveness of the IM policy by evaluating the developed algorithm against the traditional min-max policy, i. e., the (s, S) policy and the can-order policy, i.e., the (s, c, S) policy. The results in Table 3 reveal that (s, c, S) policy performs moderately well for cases with joint replenishments but independent demand scenario (on an average around 4 % cost reduction when compared with (s, S) policy). However, the IM policy with demand dependence, i.e., the (s, c, S ′ , S) policy performs extraordinarily better (on average around 36.3 % cost reduction without sacrificing service level) when compared with (s, S) policy. This is because the (s, S) policy will not take the advantage of placing joint orders for multiple medicines as well as ignore the demand dependences present among various prescribed medicines. The (s, c, S) policy considers each medication separately, but it also provides joint ordering chances whenever a medication order is placed. This makes it possible to refill the inventory at lower fixed order costs. However, the (s, c, S) policy always elevate the inventories for all medicines in the joint orders up to the order level, S. In the case of (s, c, S ′ , S) policy, demand dependence is considered among the medicines to choose the right level to bring the inventories which is the lowered order up-to level, S ′ . Overall, the (s, c, S ′ , S) policy advocate the joint ordering of associated medicines to save on ordering cost, coordinates the ordering of the multiple medicines which are prescribed together to patients and whose demand occurs at the same time, and ensures no pile up of inventories. The results align with the studies by Ahmadi et al. (2022); Feng et al. (2015); Guerrero et al. (2013); Park & Seo (2013).

Three sets of variations are made to the fixed cost, per-unit ordering cost, holding cost, and expediting cost from the starting values. In the first set, the fixed order cost and per unit ordering cost is increased in three levels (20 %, 30 % and 50 %). The results indicate that with around 20 % increase in the fixed order cost and per unit ordering cost, the inventory control policy parameter values, viz., the reorder level and can-order level are decreased by 2.89 % and 1.28 %, respectively (refer to Appendix-B Table B1 (a)). This aligns with the literature which suggests that as fixed order cost increases, it is rather better to wait until the inventory level of the medicine drops low to reorder more quantities (Feng et al., 2015;Kayis ¸ et al., 2008). This aligns with previous studies (Shah et al., 2013;Torkul et al., 2016;Uthayakumar & Karuppasamy, 2017;Yang, 2014) that suggests that since hospitals require considerable cost to hold and maintain the medicine inventory in hospitals, hence an increase in holding cost per unit leads to lower order-up-to levels. The third set increases both the holding cost and the expediting cost per unit (see Appendix-B Table B1(c)). ### 4.5. Discussion

The analysis analyses how the parameter values of the inventory control policy and total expected cost affect the performance of the suggested model. This shows that the suggested medication inventory control policy for hospitals is a promising one. Managers of hospitals should be aware that our model produces good outcomes when the stochastic components prevailing in the HSC and the knowledge on associations among prescribed medicines are utilized in a smart way. The HSC can gain from significant decreases in the inventory-related cost of pharmaceuticals by using the suggested IM approach. It was shown to be advantageous to accurately estimate patient demand for medications, demand dependences among medications and jointly replenishment of associated medicines. The suggested strategy envisions meeting the rising demand for medicines needed to treat patients, thereby lowering the possibility of inventory shortages. Additionally, the model aids in reducing the overestimation of the quantity of medications kept in HP or POCs and enhances the HSC's IM system. As a result, other hospitals will benefit since there will be enough drugs to meet demand in all the hospitals that are currently experiencing a medication shortage. Besides, inventory cost incurred by a hospital contributes around 40 % of the treatment cost (Haavik, 2000), but through smart IM systems as proposed considerable amount of savings can be achieved, thereby reducing the healthcare costs, and ensuring quality healthcare and well-being of the society.

## 5. Managerial insights and practical implications

This section suggests significant implications for hospital administration, pharmacists, inventory managers and other involved healthcare professionals. It is recommended that hospitals develop a smart inventory management system for maintaining medicines in their hospitals for treatment and care of patients. This research suggests a new approach to integrate the information of demand dependencies among medicines prescribed to patients while managing medicine inventories in the hospital pharmacy as well as point-of-care units in the hospitals. By implementing such an approach, hospitals can jointly replenish the associated medicines which need to be administered together to patients, hence ensuring availability of medicines at the time of requirement, no stock-out situations and no delay in treatment. At the same time, a joint replenishment inventory system considering medication demand dependencies is cost-effective as observed through our study findings. This proposed inventory system is effective in challenging situations in hospitals where medicines are stored in multiple locations, and patients are suffering from multiple medical conditions (multi-morbidity), thus requiring multiple medicines (polypharmacy). By developing a smart inventory management system which can identify the medicines which are frequently prescribed together to patients, estimate the demand dependencies among the associated medicines and provide optimal inventory control policy, the hospital managers can improve the performance of the medicine inventory management systems.

This research proposed a smart inventory management system which provides an optimal solution to when to place the medication order and how much order to place when medication demand is uncertain, multiple medicines are administered to patients, and inventory of those medicines are maintained in multiple locations in the hospital. Hence, this research suggests a multi-agent reinforcement learning technique incorporating medication demand uncertainties and demand dependencies among medicines and obtained optimal inventory control policy at minimum cost fulfilling the demand requirements (high service level). Since associated medicines are jointly ordered, the proposed model results in lowered order up-to-level to reduce excess inventory and expiration of medicines in the hospital along with reducing costs. Further, the cost savings can be used in other developments of hospitals, such as engaging in the digital transformation of hospitals, investing in research and development, smart operating rooms, installation of modern technologies, infrastructure improvement, and enhancing the patient-centric service (Kraus et al., 2021;Lo et al., 2019;Tortorella, Fogliatto et al., 2021, Tortorella, Saurin, et al., 2021). Besides, the knowledge of demand dependencies among medicines can be used further applied in better stock arrangements, for instance, associated medicines may be kept together to cut down on movement or transportation time (Khader et al., 2016).

Finally, the proposed model is flexible and can be easily implemented in HC systems where technology, digital transformation and sustainability is important by making minor changes in their current system (Nti et al., 2023). For example, the proposed model can be easily developed into a RL-based decision support system (Coronato et al., 2020;Padmanabhan et al., 2021) for clinical and managerial purpose by capturing patient data using Internet of medical things (IoMT), accurately capturing prescribed medicines data, estimating demand dependencies and calculate joint replenishment opportunities, suppliers availability for joint replenishment, replenishment quantities, reorder points, cost incurred and so on through technological advantage in the HSC. In fact, accurate data on current inventory level, associated medicines, and demand for medicines can be utilized further for vendor managed inventory and just-in-time strategies (Balkhi et al., 2022;Sumrit, 2021;Weraikat et al., 2019;Lee et al., 2023).
