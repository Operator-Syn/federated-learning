02The idea

What is federated learning?

Several sites train one shared model without pooling their records. Each site learns from its own data; only the updates travel back.

03Why federate?

Benefits
with asterisks.

01

Learn across sites

A small facility can benefit from patterns observed elsewhere, including events it has not seen locally.

02

Keep records local

Training can happen where records are held, helping reduce the need for centralized copies.

03

Make collaboration possible

Organizations may collaborate when moving patient-level records is difficult or unacceptable.

Federation is not free. It adds coordination, network traffic, compute at every site, and a new security obligation. Where pooling data is possible and permitted, centralized training is usually the simpler choice.
04Our context

A useful
local story.

“A facility should benefit from events it has never experienced itself.”

A low-volume barangay health station may have too few examples of a rare event to learn a reliable signal alone. Federation broadens that experience—if the right facilities can participate.

Possible tasks to explore

TB referral prioritization • follow-up / defaulter risk • commodity demand forecasting

Current thesis direction

Study inclusion-aware client selection under intermittent connectivity, with a usable health prediction task as the vehicle.

05Scenario · a coastal barangay station

Meet Ma’am
Lorna.

Midwife and officer-in-charge at a coastal barangay health station. She and one health worker maintain paper stock cards and submit monthly requisitions through the RHU.

Today

Seasonal demand surprises her. She over-orders some months, then still risks running out of essentials.

With a useful model

A short, explainable forecast helps pre-fill the requisition she already completes. No new dashboard or training ritual.

Federation’s role

Signals learned from other facilities may improve her forecast—especially for patterns absent from her own records.

The open question. Does this match the decision the station actually makes, and can it be made from records it already keeps? The health office answers both.
06What makes it hard?

Participation
is the problem.

Barangay facilities differ in bandwidth, power stability, staff availability, and local data volume. A “fastest clients first” policy may finish sooner—but repeatedly leave weaker links out.

Efficiency

How quickly does training reach a pre-defined useful target? How many bytes and rounds does it cost?

Inclusion

Who participates, who is starved of rounds, and how does each facility perform—not just the pooled average?

Study question: Which selection policy reaches the target quickly without pushing any facility below a participation floor?

07What is required?

Small start.
Real constraints.

Software

  • Local, structured data and a documented schema
  • Training code + compatible model at each client
  • Coordinator/server to schedule rounds and aggregate updates
  • Monitoring, authentication, authorization, audit, secure transport
  • Preprocessing and site-level evaluation

Hardware & access

  • CPU may suffice for a small tabular model; GPU depends on model and scale
  • Enough local storage/RAM for data and training batches
  • Coordinator needs dependable compute and reachable clients
  • Intermittent links require retries, timeouts, and participation policies
  • Digitization, export rights, staff time, and support are often the harder prerequisites
Where this study sits. Evaluation runs in a controlled simulation, with network assumptions measured where possible. A field deployment would also need facility access, working infrastructure, and governance agreements in place.
08Platform direction

NVIDIA FLARE
over CODA?

We are evaluating NVIDIA FLARE as the training and orchestration layer for these experiments. CODA remains an important healthcare precedent, but the two are not interchangeable.

Why FLARE is a candidate

A general-purpose FL framework with client/server workflows and simulation, suited to experiments on selection, availability, and training rounds.

What to verify first

Can it expose the controls and logs for custom selection, client dropouts, timeouts, per-site metrics, and repeatable runs? Its versioned docs set the real requirements.

What is being compared. FLARE is tested as the training and orchestration layer. It supplies no data mapping, privacy, or facility access, and CODA is a healthcare federation architecture. The comparison is against our requirements.
